\section{Lessons learned}
The completion of this final degree project has validated the feasibility of integrating vision language models (VLMs) as high-level orchestrators in complex multi-agent robotic environments. Following the research, development, and experimental phases, the following key conclusions and lessons have been identified:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Effectiveness of architectural modularity}: it has been demonstrated that for tasks requiring precision and logical coordination, the use of inverse kinematics and deterministic controllers offers a level of reliability and operational safety that current RL policies struggle to achieve in resource-constrained environments. Moreover, the modular architecture allows a much better control over the components the system is composed of, such as the VLM server or the controller functions. 
	
	\item \textbf{Vision language models stochasticity}: VLMs have demonstrated a variability in their spatial predictions, including noise and hallucination. The integration multiple prompts and calculating the median of the predicted coordinates proved to be a more robust solution. The helping additional prompts resulted to obtain better results as the VLM would not rely on a single prompt, but rather a collection of similar prompts where it could extract more valuable information from. 
	
	\item \textbf{Challenges of asynchronous orchestration and the integrated physics engine}: the coordination of a Franka robotic arm and the Jetbot mobile platform highlighted the importance of asynchronous programming in robotics.  Furthermore, the strict physics engine integrated into Isaac Sim reflected the importance of the correct synchronization calls and logic. 
	
	\item \textbf{The role of hardware in modern physics simulators}: working with  Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab revealed that physical and visual fidelity come with an extremely high computational cost. Optimizing VRAM and CPU usage was not merely a technical step but a necessity to allow the inference server and the simulator to coexist while balancing the performance of the model and agents, underscoring the need for powerful and clear infrastructure in modern robotic AI development.
	
	\item \textbf{Versatility of vision language models in robotics}: finally, it is concluded that the use of natural language to guide robots drastically lowers the entry barrier for programming complex tasks. This project establishes a solid foundation for future systems where human operators can reconfigure multi-agent workflows simply by describing the task, eliminating the need for manual trajectory reprogramming.
\end{itemize}

In summary, while the path towards fully autonomous and unified models (VLA) still presents significant technical challenges, the integration of VLMs with classical controllers represents a highly potent hybrid solution for service robotics.

\section{Limitations found}
The main drawbacks I have found during the development and research process during the whole work have been mainly hardware-related as the specific software requirements necessary to carry on the development are severely demanding due to the fact that the majority of the processes are run and rendered in the GPU. Although I have had access to a good graphical processing unit, the other components of the my working device have fallen behind. Taking for instance the minimum requirements of the used framework \textit{Isaac Sim}, I found myself in the limits of the bare minimum, and some of my components such as the CPU were worse than the required. However, I have been able to carry on anyways although there have been a few moments where the I have really felt the bottleneck. When it comes those moments I have felt limited and slowed downed due to my equipment are the following:

\begin{itemize}
	\item I have needed to split some of the working components of the pipeline process inside \textit{Isaac Sim} as the whole process would not fit in my GPU's VRAM. The simple fact of opening the framework already consumed close to a quarter of the graphic card's total capacity, and starting the server to listen and interact with the VLM calls alongside left little to no room for more processes in the background. The fact of working on the edge of the minimum hardware requirements also slowed the rendering of visual work and reduced the number of frames per second, nevertheless, this did not obstruct the development process.
	
	\item When it comes to making the most of the reinforcement learning training process, I have needed to tweak some of the predefined system configurations to maximize the performance and reduce any kind of lagging and overflow. Adapting the GPU capacity properties to maximize the overall functioning. If I were to work with better equipment, I would have liked to go for longer and deeper trainings.
	
	\item Not all limitations have been hardware related, as the official NVIDIA documentation websites were usually corrupted depending on the version. Leading to consulting external pages to solve the mismatching dependencies or installation processes for the frameworks used. After consulting the official documentation of the \textit{Isaac Lab} repository, I found that even the default reinforcement learning training examples and demonstrations did not work as intended independently of the version. I was not the only one with the same issues as other peoples raised the same issues in different forums, so I inspired on the solutions given by the users with the same problems. In the bibliography, the discussions forums can be found related to this topic (\cite{GithubLiftQuestion}, \cite{NVIDIAFrankaQuestion}). 
\end{itemize}

\section{Future work}
There are several improvement ideas that could be carried in a future work. Even if this project establishes the foundations of vision language models implementation into robotic environments, there are still several things that could have gone better and new ambitious strategies that are worth considering for the future research.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Sim2Real transfer}: the whole development and testing processes have been carried in a controlled simulator environment. However, there is a huge gap between simulated environments and real life deployment. There are infinite things that may work in the simulated environment but work totally different in the real testing. Vision language models would have to deal with more visual alterations such as different illumination and noise in the image sensors and the agent controllers would have to take into account real physics. Furthermore, the actual deployment and testing of multi-agent robotic environments is much more expensive.
	
	\item \textbf{Stable RL policy development}: the instability of the reinforcement learning policies has been evident throughout the whole development of the study. A future work on the RL topic would include the successful training of the policies, and a correct inference performance on real scenarios. Strategies such as \textit{curriculum} and \textit{imitation learning} could be also be explored. Other deep reinforcement learning algorithms are reported to work better in robotic tasks, \textit{SAC (Soft Actor Critic)} is one of them. However, it is not natively supported in Isaac Lab but it would still be interesting to develop its implementation to compare the performance and stability to other algorithms such as the used PPO. 
	
	\item \textbf{SOTA VLM integration}: the current computational power has led me to use quantized VLMs and balance performance with efficiency. If disposing of higher computational power, more powerful vision language models could be tested, models with a much better performance. And perhaps would eliminate the need of integrating the helping techniques such as CV filters and helping offsets that have been used in the project for empowering the model's capacity. 
	
	\item \textbf{Vision language action model implementation}: the VLA have been mentioned in the SOTA chapter of the documentation (section \ref{sec:VLA}), highlighting their huge potential even if they are still in their early and immature development phases. Nevertheless, once they reach a solid foundational base, their implementation in robotic tasks would be the most dominant. Their architecture, compared to the VLM architecture used in the project, enables a direct vision to action integration. A future research with VLAs would involve the substitution of the VLM and custom controller classes for the implementation of a vision language action that would be able to analyze the environment and return the most adequate action at each time. Removing the need for complex coordinate transformation calculations and fixed movement coordination functions. Nevertheless, these models are still far from being implemented and tested in real environments, but could become predominant once they are mature enough. 
\end{itemize}
